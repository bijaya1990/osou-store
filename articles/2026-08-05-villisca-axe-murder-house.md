# The Villisca Axe Murder House: America's Most Disturbing Unsolved Crime — and the House That Never Let It Go

On a warm June night in 1912, eight people walked home from a church program in a small Iowa farming town. Six of them were children. By sunrise, every single one of them was dead.

No one was ever convicted. The murder weapon was the family's own axe. And the house where it happened is still standing today — restored, preserved, and open to anyone brave enough to spend the night in it.

This is the story of the Villisca Axe Murder House.

---

## The Night of June 9, 1912

Villisca, Iowa was a town of roughly 2,500 people, the kind of place where nobody locked their doors. Josiah Moore, 43, ran a farm implement business. He and his wife Sarah, 39, had four children: Herman, 11; Katherine, 10; Boyd, 7; and Paul, 5.

That Sunday evening, the family attended the Children's Day program at the Presbyterian church. Sarah had helped organize it. Katherine had invited two friends, sisters Ina Stillinger, 8, and Lena Stillinger, 11, to sleep over afterward rather than walk home in the dark.

The program ended around 9:30 p.m. The ten-minute walk home was the last time anyone saw the Moore family alive.

Sometime between midnight and 5:00 a.m., someone entered the house.

---

## What the Killer Left Behind

The crime scene at 508 East 2nd Street was, and remains, one of the strangest ever documented in American criminal history. It was not simply a murder. It was a scene that someone had *arranged*.

Every victim had been struck repeatedly in the head with an axe — Josiah's own axe, taken from his own yard. Investigators found it propped in the downstairs guest bedroom, still bloodied.

But it was everything *else* that unsettled the men who walked through that house on the morning of June 10:

- **Every mirror in the house had been covered** with blankets, clothing, or whatever fabric was at hand.
- **The victims' faces were covered** with bedsheets after they were killed.
- **A four-pound slab of bacon**, taken from the icebox, was lying on the floor beside the axe.
- **The keyhole of the front door was plugged** with a piece of cloth cut from Josiah's own coat.
- **A bowl of bloody water** sat on the kitchen table, next to a plate of uneaten food.
- **Kerosene lamps had their glass chimneys removed** and the wicks turned down low, and were found positioned at the foot of the beds — as though the killer had wanted just enough light to work by, and no more.
- **Two spent cigarettes lay on the attic floor**, suggesting the killer had waited up there, above the sleeping family, for hours.

Josiah was struck so many times that his face was unrecognizable and the axe blade left gouges in the ceiling above the bed.

Lena Stillinger, the eldest of the two visiting sisters, was found lying crosswise on the downstairs bed with a defensive wound on her arm — the only sign that any of the eight had woken up.

---

## The Morning It Was Discovered

At around 7:00 a.m., neighbor Mary Peckham noticed something wrong. The Moores were early risers, and the chores hadn't been done. The chickens hadn't been let out. Nothing moved.

She knocked. No answer. She tried the door — locked, which was itself unusual.

Peckham called Ross Moore, Josiah's brother. He had a spare key. He let himself into the parlor, opened the door of the downstairs guest bedroom, and saw the two Stillinger girls in the bed. He didn't go any further. He walked back outside and told Peckham to call the sheriff.

By 8:40 a.m., the town telephone operator had alerted the whole community. And that is where the investigation was lost.

---

## How the Case Was Destroyed Before It Began

Modern true-crime audiences tend to assume the Villisca case went cold because 1912 policing lacked forensic tools. That's only half the story. The real catastrophe was crowd control.

Before the county coroner arrived at roughly 9:00 a.m., **dozens of townspeople had already walked through the house.** The sheriff couldn't hold them back. Neighbors filed through the bedrooms to look at the bodies. The axe was handled by, according to later accounts, hundreds of people.

Some visitors took souvenirs. Fragments of Josiah Moore's skull were reportedly carried out of the house.

The National Guard finally secured the property around 10:30 a.m. — more than three hours after discovery. The bodies weren't removed to the makeshift morgue at the fire station until after 10:00 p.m. that night, nearly a full day after the murders.

Whatever the killer left behind, the town erased.

---

## The Suspects Who Were Never Convicted

The Villisca investigation dragged on for nearly a decade and produced grand jury hearings, a slander suit, a murder trial, and a great deal of ruined reputations. Four names dominate.

### Reverend George Kelly

An English-born traveling minister who had attended the very Children's Day program the Moores went to. He left Villisca on an early train between 5:00 and 5:50 a.m. on the morning the bodies were found.

Kelly was, by most accounts, mentally unwell — he was arrested in 1914 for mailing obscene material. Under lengthy interrogation he confessed to the murders, then recanted. He was tried twice. The first trial ended in a hung jury. The second, in 1917, ended in acquittal. Jurors simply did not believe the confession.

### Frank F. Jones

A Villisca businessman and sitting Iowa state senator. Josiah Moore had worked for Jones for years before leaving to open a competing implement business — taking the lucrative John Deere dealership with him. There were persistent rumors of a personal grievance as well.

The theory was that Jones hired someone to kill Moore. No evidence ever directly implicated him, but the suspicion followed him for the rest of his life and poisoned the town for a generation.

### William "Blackie" Mansfield

An ex-convict named by private investigator James Wilkerson as a serial axe murderer responsible for Villisca and several similar crimes. Mansfield had a documented alibi — payroll records placed him in Illinois. He was released and successfully sued Wilkerson for slander, winning $2,225.

### Paul Mueller and "The Man from the Train"

The most compelling modern theory. In 2017, baseball statistician and author Bill James and his daughter Rachel McCarthy James published *The Man from the Train*, arguing that Villisca was one killing in a long series committed by a German immigrant named Paul Mueller who traveled by rail and struck near train lines.

The pattern-matching is genuinely striking. Consider what happened in the months around Villisca:

| Location | Date | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| Colorado Springs, CO | Sept 1911 | Two families killed with an axe |
| Ellsworth, KS | 1911 | Family killed, axe, covered faces |
| Paola, KS | June 5, 1912 | Family of six, axe — **four days before Villisca** |

Covered mirrors. Covered faces. Dimmed lamps. Axes taken from the victims' own property. Houses within walking distance of railroad tracks.

Whether Mueller was the man or not, the evidence that Villisca was not an isolated crime is hard to dismiss.

**The case has never been officially closed.**

## The House Today

For most of the 20th century, the house was simply a rental property. People lived there. Families raised children in the rooms where the Moores died.

In 1994, historians Darwin and Martha Linn bought the property and did something unusual: they *un-renovated* it. They tore out the indoor plumbing. They removed the electrical wiring. They restored the wallpaper, the fixtures, and the layout to match exactly how the house looked on the night of June 9, 1912.

On **December 1, 1997**, the property was added to the National Register of Historic Places as the Josiah B. and Sara Moore House.

Today it operates as a museum and a paranormal destination — and the lack of electricity is not a gimmick. There are no lights. Overnight guests navigate by lantern and flashlight, in the same darkness the killer worked in.

## Is the Villisca Axe Murder House Actually Haunted?

Here is where an honest article has to separate two things: what is documented, and what is reported.

**What is documented:** eight murders, an unsolved case, a preserved house, and one very strange modern incident.

**What is reported** — consistently, by guests, guides, tour operators, and television crews for over three decades:

- Children's voices and laughter from the upstairs bedrooms
- Disembodied footsteps crossing the floor above occupied rooms
- Objects moving, particularly children's toys left as offerings
- Doors opening and closing on their own
- Shadow figures in doorways and at the top of the stairs
- Falling objects with no source
- Sudden, overwhelming waves of grief, dread, or rage — reported even on **daytime** tours, by visitors who came skeptical

The house has been investigated on national television repeatedly, including *Ghost Adventures* (Season 4) and *The Dead Files*. Investigators have reported EVP recordings and EMF anomalies throughout the structure, particularly in the upstairs children's bedroom and the attic where the cigarettes were found.

### The 2014 Incident

This one is not folklore. It's in the police record.

On **November 7, 2014**, shortly after midnight, a group of paranormal investigators was staying in the house overnight. Among them was **Robert Laursen Jr., 37, of Rhinelander, Wisconsin.** He had brought a hunting knife into the house — over the caretaker's explicit warning not to.

Laursen went upstairs alone. The others heard screaming.

They found him on the floor of an upstairs bedroom with multiple stab wounds to his chest. He was airlifted to a hospital in Omaha and survived.

The wounds were self-inflicted. Laursen said afterward that he remembered a flash of light and then waking up in the hospital, with no memory of what happened in between. Police told reporters they didn't believe it was an accident, but no charges were filed and no explanation was ever established.

Whatever you believe about the paranormal, something happened in that room that nobody has been able to account for.

## How to Visit the Villisca Axe Murder House

**Address:** 508 East 2nd Street, Villisca, Iowa 50864 — signposted locally as the Villisca Ax Murder House, spelled without the "e" on most Iowa tourism listings.
**Getting there:** Villisca sits in southwest Iowa, about 90 minutes southeast of Omaha, Nebraska; roughly two hours southwest of Des Moines; and about two and a half hours north of Kansas City. It's an easy add-on to an I-29 or I-80 road trip. The town is small — the house is in an ordinary residential neighborhood, and people still live on the street.

**Daytime tours:** Guided walkthroughs run in the afternoon during the operating season. Private group tours are available (currently around $300 for up to ten people, with additional guests at $20 each). Check the official site before driving out — hours are seasonal and weather-dependent.

**Overnight stays:** This is the main draw. You can book a single room (around $199) or the entire house. Bookings run from evening until early morning, and the overnight is explicitly *not* sold as accommodation — it's an experience. There is no electricity, no running water, and no plumbing. Bring your own lighting, water, and warm layers.

**Booking:** Reserve through the official site at murderhouse.com or by phone. **October sells out months in advance.** If you want a Halloween-week night, book by summer.

**Please note:** The site asks visitors to remember that eight real people — six of them children — died in this house. Provoking, taunting, or disrespectful behavior is not welcome, and as 2014 demonstrated, it has consequences.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What happened at the Villisca Axe Murder House?**
On the night of June 9–10, 1912, an unknown intruder killed eight people — Josiah and Sarah Moore, their four children, and two young houseguests — with an axe while they slept. The crime was never solved.

**Who killed the Moore family?**
No one was ever convicted. Reverend George Kelly was tried twice and acquitted. Other suspects included state senator Frank F. Jones, ex-convict William Mansfield, and a traveling killer proposed in 2017 by author Bill James. The case remains open.

**Can you stay overnight at the Villisca Axe Murder House?**
Yes. The house offers overnight stays by the room or by the whole house. There is no electricity or plumbing — guests use lanterns and flashlights, exactly as it would have been in 1912.

**How much does it cost to visit?**
Daytime tours are inexpensive and available seasonally; private tours start around $300 for a group of ten. Overnight room rentals run around $199. Prices change, so confirm with the official site.

**Is the Villisca Axe Murder House real?**
Yes. The murders are a matter of historical record and the house is listed on the National Register of Historic Places as the Josiah B. and Sara Moore House, added December 1, 1997.

**Why were the mirrors covered?**
No one knows. Covering mirrors was a period mourning custom in some communities, but the killer covered them *before* or *during* the murders, not after — and the same detail appears in several other unsolved axe murders from the same era, which is part of why some researchers believe a single traveling killer was responsible.

## The Part That Stays With You

Most famous American hauntings come with an asterisk. Amityville was substantially fabricated. Many "most haunted" locations have thin or invented histories.

Villisca has the opposite problem. Nothing about it needs embellishing.

There is a house, still standing, restored to the hour. There are eight names on gravestones in the Villisca Cemetery. There is a case file that was never closed. And there is a small Iowa town that spent the following decade tearing itself apart with accusations, because everyone knew that whoever did it had probably sat in the same church pew they had.

You don't have to believe in ghosts to find that unbearable.
