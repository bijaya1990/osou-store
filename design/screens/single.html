The Gettysburg Recordings: What Six Nights of Audio Actually Captured

A team left forty-one hours of unattended audio running across the Triangular Field. We had every minute independently analyzed — and the results are stranger than the footage.

By Marta Vance
Gettysburg, Pennsylvania · October 26, 2025 · 11 min read

The equipment went in on a Tuesday, in daylight, with permission. Six recorders, each sealed, each on its own timer, spread across roughly four hundred yards of the Triangular Field and the treeline above it. Nobody stayed with them. That was the point.

Every previous recording made here has one thing in common: a person was holding the microphone. Breath, fabric, footsteps on dry leaves, the low involuntary noises people make when they are frightened in the dark — all of it lands in the audio, and all of it gets interpreted later as something else. Remove the investigator and you remove most of the argument.

What came back was forty-one hours and eighteen minutes of usable audio. We sent all of it, unedited and unlabeled, to a forensic audio lab in Pittsburgh that had never been told where it came from.

What the lab was asked to do

The brief was deliberately narrow. Flag every event above the ambient floor that could not be attributed to wind, wildlife, aircraft, or distant road noise. Do not speculate about cause. Do not attempt transcription of anything that is not clearly speech.

Nineteen events came back flagged. Thirteen of them resolved on second pass: a deer at 2:14 a.m. on the fourth night, a low-flying medical helicopter, a fence line under thermal stress as the temperature dropped through the mid-forties.

Six events remain unattributed. That is not the same as saying six events are unexplainable.

Dr. Aaron Whitfield, forensic audio analyst

The six that are left

Three of the six occurred within an eleven-minute window on the second night, on recorders roughly two hundred yards apart. That detail matters more than the sound itself. A single device catching something odd is a device. Three devices catching something in sequence, along a line, is a direction of travel.

The remaining three are individually unremarkable and collectively difficult. Each is a short, low-frequency percussive event with no decay profile matching anything in the lab's reference library — not a footfall, not a branch, not a gunshot echo from the range eleven miles southwest.

What this does not prove

Nothing here demonstrates the presence of anything. Six unattributed events across forty-one hours in an open field bordered by woodland is, statistically, an unremarkable result. The park is loud in ways that do not sound loud to a person standing in it.

What the recordings do establish is narrower and more useful: the most-cited audio evidence from this site has never been gathered under conditions that would let anyone rule out the investigator. Now some of it has been.
